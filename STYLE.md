# Style Guide

The target register: broadly contemporary commercial fantasy/SF craft in the
Brandon Sanderson zone — not a style copy, but that neighborhood. Clear,
propulsive prose in service of story; strong action writing; tone that is
neither grim nor light; deep, consistent lore delivered without infodumps.
Read this before every drafting session.

## Prose fundamentals

- **Transparent prose.** The sentence should disappear behind what it shows.
  Vivid over ornate; concrete over abstract. Purple prose is a defect.
- **Contemporary diction**, but no modern slang or anachronistic idiom in a
  secondary world. Characters can say "okay" was invented for our world —
  find the in-world equivalent register.
- **Vary sentence rhythm deliberately.** Short sentences for impact and
  action. Longer ones for reflection and atmosphere. If three consecutive
  sentences share a shape, break one.
- **Strong verbs, few adverbs.** "She slammed the door" not "she closed the
  door forcefully."
- **Sensory grounding.** Every scene establishes place through at least two
  senses within the first few paragraphs — through the POV character's
  attention, not a camera pan.

## Point of view

- **Third person limited**, one POV character per chapter (or per scene, with
  a clear scene break `* * *`). No head-hopping — ever.
- The narration is colored by the POV character: what they notice, what they
  compare things to, what vocabulary they'd use. A soldier and a scholar
  describe the same room differently.
- Direct interior thought is used sparingly, in italics, present tense:
  *That door wasn't locked an hour ago.*
- Keep the POV roster small (3–5 primary POVs unless the outline justifies
  more). Each POV thread must have its own arc, not just a camera position.

## Scene and chapter craft

- **Every scene has a job**: it changes a character's situation, knowledge, or
  relationships. A scene that only delivers lore or only shows travel gets cut
  or merged. Most scenes should do two or more jobs at once.
- **Enter late, leave early.** Start as close to the interesting part as the
  reader can follow; end on the turn, not on the winding-down.
- **Chapters end with a pull**: a question, a reversal, a decision, a door
  opening. Not a cliffhanger every time — but never a full stop where the
  reader can comfortably leave.
- **Scene-sequel rhythm.** After intense action or revelation, give the POV
  character (and reader) a beat to process — reaction, dilemma, decision. This
  is where character lives. Don't chain set pieces back to back.

## Action writing

- **Legible geography.** Before a fight or chase, the reader knows the space:
  exits, obstacles, distances, who is where. During it, track positions
  precisely. Never let choreography go vague to sound exciting.
- **Action is character.** How someone fights — cautious, showy, brutal,
  improvisational — expresses who they are. Two characters never fight the
  same way.
- **Cost and consequence.** Injuries persist (log them in continuity notes).
  Exhaustion accumulates. Victories cost something.
- Short paragraphs and sentences in the thick of it. Slow motion is for the
  single decisive moment, not the whole fight.
- Any speculative powers used in a fight obey their established rules under
  pressure — pressure is exactly when the rules must hold.

## Lore and worldbuilding on the page

- **Iceberg principle.** The bible knows ten times what the page says. Detail
  reaches the page only when a character touches it: uses it, argues about it,
  is inconvenienced by it.
- **No infodumps.** Never pause the story to explain. History arrives through
  dialogue with stakes, through objects, through what characters take for
  granted. Two in-world sentences beat a paragraph of narrator lecture.
- **Trust the reader.** Introduce terms in context without immediate
  definition; let usage teach. Readers in this genre enjoy decoding.
- **Consistency is the payoff.** Lore details planted early should click into
  place later. That click — "it was there all along" — is a core pleasure of
  the genre we're honoring. Track every plant in `bible/plot/promises.md`.

## Dialogue

- Every named character has a **recognizable voice**: sentence length,
  vocabulary, directness, humor. Test: strip the tags — can you still tell
  who's speaking?
- Dialogue is action: characters want things from each other and rarely say
  exactly what they mean. Pure exposition exchanges ("As you know...") are
  banned.
- Tags: mostly `said`/`asked`. Action beats over adverbs ("He didn't look up
  from the blade. 'Try again.'").

## Tone

- **Not grim, not light.** Stakes are real, losses are permanent, and the
  story still believes in courage, loyalty, and earned hope. Darkness exists
  to be pushed against.
- Humor comes from character and situation, not from the narrator winking at
  the reader. Banter is fine; relentless quipping that deflates tension is not.
- Sincerity over irony. Characters are allowed to care openly about things.

## The edit pass (mandatory, after every chapter draft)

Author-mandated (2026-07-31). Drafting a chapter produces a *draft*; the
chapter is not done until it has survived an edit pass with two lenses.
Do the pass as a genuine re-read of the full chapter, not a skim, and make
the fixes before any bookkeeping.

**Lens 1 — writing quality:**
- Every scene doing its job(s); enter late / leave early actually observed.
- POV discipline: nothing on the page the POV character wouldn't notice or
  phrase that way.
- **Metaphor audit:** each metaphor must be (a) consistent — no mixing
  within a passage, (b) meaningful — drawn from the POV character's world
  and doing characterizing work, (c) fresh — if it could be pasted into any
  other book, cut or replace it. Metaphors are rationed: one working image
  beats three decorative ones.
- Rhythm: read paragraphs aloud in the mind's ear; break runs of same-shape
  sentences; check paragraph-length variety across the chapter.
- Continuity spot-check against bible/notes for every fact asserted.

**Lens 2 — AI smell.** Constructions that mark machine prose. Hunt and kill:
- **Stock phrasing:** "couldn't help but," "a mixture of X and Y,"
  "somewhere between X and Y," "not for the first time," "let out a breath
  he didn't know he was holding," "the air was thick with," "a testament
  to," "unbidden," "impossibly," "a small eternity," "something in his
  chest," "something shifted," "the weight of," eyes widening, knuckles
  whitening, breath catching.
- **Tic constructions:** "Not X. Not Y. But Z." fragment ladders;
  one-word dramatic paragraphs ("Alive."); anaphora three or more deep;
  rhetorical-question chains; "It was only later that…"; paired
  abstractions ("grief and something like hope").
- **Over-signaling:** emotions named instead of enacted; a closing line
  that files the scene's meaning for the reader; adjectives doing the work
  verbs should; every noun escorted by a modifier.
- **Uniformity:** same scene-opening move twice in one chapter (weather →
  interiority → dialogue, etc.); dialogue exchanges that alternate at
  identical lengths; every beat landing at paragraph-end.
- **Tonal flattening:** the pass must confirm the chapter's intended tone
  (dread, comedy, grief, action) is *distinct* from the neighboring
  chapters' — check the last two chapters' textures and differ.

Additions to this list discovered during drafting go in
`notes/decisions-log.md` under "Prose ban-list additions" and get checked
thereafter.

## Known failure modes — actively resist

These are the specific ways LLM-drafted long fiction goes wrong. Check every
chapter against this list.

1. **Scene compression.** Rushing through what should be a full dramatized
   scene in a paragraph of summary. If the outline beat matters, it gets a
   scene with dialogue and grounding. Chapters land in the 3,000–4,500 word
   range because the scenes are *full*, not because prose is padded.
2. **Crutch phrases and tics.** Watch for and cut: "couldn't help but,"
   "a mixture of X and Y," "despite himself," eyes that "widen" constantly,
   breath that is forever "caught" or "released," "the weight of," "something
   shifted," knuckles whitening. Maintain a growing ban-list in
   `notes/decisions-log.md` as tics are noticed.
3. **Over-summarized endings.** Do not close chapters with a tidy paragraph
   restating what the events meant. End in the scene.
4. **Moralizing narrator.** The narration never explains the theme.
5. **Everyone sounds smart and reasonable.** Let characters be wrong, petty,
   biased, and unaware of it. Conflict between sympathetic people beats
   villain monologues.
6. **Forgetting the body.** POV characters keep having physical states —
   hunger, cold, old injuries — that persist across scene breaks.
7. **Convenience drift.** Locks, distances, guard schedules, and power limits
   quietly bending to help the plot. When the plot needs something to be easy,
   make it cost something else instead.
8. **Uniform pacing.** Some chapters should be fast and short-sentenced; some
   slow and thick. If any three consecutive chapters have the same texture,
   restructure.
