# Chapter Three — The Levy Scout

Somebody had been lying up in the bracken above the top paddock, and they had been at it for days.

Joss found the place on the eighth morning after the Inning, gone up before breakfast to see the wether flock settled on its new ground. At the lip of the old quarry-scrape, where the hill kept the wind off and the bracken stood waist-high, a shape the length of a person had been pressed into the growth — pressed and half sprung back, the way bracken goes when it has been lain on more than once and given up arguing about it. He crouched and worked his fingers down through the fronds into the litter beneath. Dry to the bottom, and everything else on that slope grey to the knee with dew. Whoever it was had been in place before the dew fell and gone after it dried, this morning and other mornings besides. His first thought was poacher, and it lasted as long as it took him to look where the flattened bracken looked: not up at the warrens, not down at the mere where the fowl came in of an evening. He put himself into the shape of it to be certain, knees in the cold litter, chin at the bracken's edge, and found his own life laid out below him like a counting-board — yard and barn and byre, the muck-heap steaming in the early air, his mother crossing from the hen-house with her apron gathered up, and in the near paddock the old cob and the young gelding grazing head-down, side by side, the assessment chalk on the gelding's shoulder weathered by now to a pale ghost of itself.

"You walk heavy on the skyline," said a voice behind him. "I heard you from the fold."

He came up and around all at once, one boot skidding in the litter, and had to catch himself on his knuckles, which took most of the dignity out of anything he might have said.

She stood a dozen feet up the slope with the early sun at her shoulder, so that looking at her meant squinting — and he understood, even as he squinted, that this was not where she happened to be standing but where she had chosen to stand, and he filed that away with a kind of sour respect. Lean and weathered, dark hair pulled back in a tail, a short heavy coat the color of the dead bracken it had been lying in. A crossbow rode across her body on its sling with the settled look of a thing its owner had stopped noticing years ago. She was younger than her stillness — two-and-twenty, near enough — and she was looking at him exactly as he had just been looking at the pressed bracken: as sign. A thing to be read, measured, and moved past.

"That's Fenn ground you've been lying on," he said.

"Then the bracken's yours." Her voice was level, unhurried, giving nothing away to the slope between them. "Do you want it stood back up?"

Rather than answer that, he looked at her properly, the whole kit of her: the quarrels fletched in grey goose, a knife worn to a sliver with sharpening, and at her belt a peeled hazel stick as long as his forearm, notched along one edge in close-set groups.

"You're the scout, then. Billeted at the reeve's." The parish had known for three days that the shire had set a scout on Reeve Chessom's household, and had known it the way the parish knew anything, which was at once and wrong in most particulars. She had been a Marches woman, a king's ranger, the forerider of a column that would eat the district bare — depending on whose gate you leaned on while you heard it. Nobody had said young. Nobody had said she'd spend her mornings lying in Fenn bracken.

"And you're the middle Fenn," she said. "The one that stays."

That went in quick and neat as a fish-hook, and he judged it wasn't meant to — it was an item off a list, said the way you'd say a field was in barley. Elder son written for the levy; middle son stays; a girl. Somewhere between the reeve's fireside and three mornings on this hill, the Fenns of Fennstead had been counted, all five, and set down each in their place, and this was his: the one that stays.

"Joss," he said.

She didn't trade him her name for it. She looked past him, down the slope, at the paddock. "The young horse is the marked one."

It wasn't a question, but he answered it. "Marked at cutting. Cropp's man chalked him. If the trains came up short in the autumn, they said—"

"They're short now."

She came down the slope to his level as she said it, moving not on the sheep-paths but just beside them, on ground that didn't speak underfoot.

"You had a fire, nine days back. The toll yards."

"I know what burned." It came out shorter than he meant it to. The fire was the parish's; they had watched it from their own roofs; he found he didn't care to have it handed back to him by a stranger, itemized.

"Then you know it was the remount hay. A winter's feeding for the levy's horse-trains, gone in a night. Horses eat, Fenn — that's the first thing about horses and the last. The toll can't winter them now, so the trains make up early, and they make up south at Marle where there's fodder standing, and the marked beasts in the district books are called in to fill the strings. The party's a day behind me. I came ahead to walk the ground."

He turned that over, and while he turned it she waited, with no impatience he could find anywhere on her.

"Called in," he said. "Taken."

"Bought. There's a chit."

"At the crown's price."

"At the crown's price."

Nothing in her face put a shine on it, and nothing apologized for it either; it was one more true thing about horses. Below them the gelding had drifted along the paddock rail, cropping, the mark on his shoulder showing and hiding with his stride, and Joss thought of Davin at the Inning reaching for the cup he'd sworn off. *Mind you keep the gelding fed, they've written us down together.* Forty-three days. And the horse going first.

"Three mornings," Joss said. "For two horses a man could count from the lane. What were you counting the other two?"

Interest moved behind her face — not warmth; the way a buyer's face moves at market when a beast walks sounder than its price. "First morning tells you what's there," she said. "Second, what moves, and when, and who moves it. The third morning is for what they'd hide, and where, if word ran ahead of the party. Word always runs ahead of the party."

"You were waiting to see if we'd walk him off."

"Mostly folk do. There's a mare stood in the marl-pit shaw this morning three fields from the ground she was bred on. There's a red heifer in the alder carr below the mill that nobody drives to water in daylight." She said it without any heat at all, the way Wystan would say a letter had gone quiet. "Your father left the marked horse standing in plain sight of the hill. That's rarer than you'd think."

"And the mare and the heifer. Are they on your stick?"

She glanced down at the notched hazel at her belt, and then at him, and for the first time she seemed to look at him rather than read him.

"My stick's my own," she said. "I'm paid to bring a drove through soft country with the book's beasts on it. Nobody's paid me to make the parish honest." She turned to go up the slope, and then gave him the rest over her shoulder, flat as a man handing back change. "Chalk washes off. The book doesn't. If your father means to argue, tell him to argue with the paper at the green, not with the drovers at his gate. It goes worse with the drovers."

"Is there any arguing with the paper?"

"You can argue."

She went up the way she'd come down, unhurried, keeping off the paths, and the coat did its work and had her gone against the hillside sooner than his eyes would agree to. Joss stood a while by the flattened place where a stranger had spent three mornings learning his family, and then went down to breakfast to tell them, and found on the way that every shape he tried the telling in came out sounding like the counting-board: yard, barn, byre, beasts, and every one of them written.

*  *  *

Bram heard it through without once stopping eating, which was itself an answer, and when the bread was done he said, "He'll go clean, then," and went out, and that was the whole of the Fenn family council. Lise asked the questions that could be used — when the party came, what was paid, whether the chit was good at Ferrand or only good for waving — and Petra asked the one that couldn't: what the stick was for, if the book was the count. Nobody had an answer to give her, so she folded it away with the rest of her collection.

The party came up the low road in the middle of the afternoon: Serjeant Cropp on a heavy cob with his bad leg out of the stirrup, a clerk with a writing-board on his saddlebow, and two drovers hung about with spare headstalls — and no soldiers, which the parish noted with relief and, in the same breath, a little insult. At dusk the reeve cried the parish to the green.

The green at nightfall smelled of the year turning: wood-smoke sitting low, mist coming up off the mere to lie along the hedge-bottoms, dew already thick on the grass. A lantern hung on the moot-barn front threw the clerk's shadow tall across the wall while he read, and behind the crowd, at the green's far end, the mounds of the old levy butts sat grass-grown in the dark, from the years when statute shooting was kept and nobody living remembered why it had stopped.

Nine beasts of the parish were written in the book. The clerk read them slowly, by lantern-light, with the reeve holding the lantern and looking as though he would rather have held anything else: the smith's dray mare; Carter's road pair, that carted for half the district; a riding cob of the Chessoms' own, which bought the reeve a little credit at least; and, along with the rest, the bay gelding, four years, of Fennstead. Josten Aldery said one word from the smith's bench-end, low, and his neighbors' silence agreed with it. Bram stood at the back with his thumbs in his belt and heard the list to its end, and then walked home before the clerk had finished with the hours and the halters, and men stepped back to let him through without a word said on either side.

It might have gone sour after that — the green had the makings, an evening's anger and a short list of horses to spend it on — and what it went instead was sideways, because of the scout.

She had been there through the reading — Pike, her name was, Renna Pike; the reeve's wife had had it around the parish inside a day, since the scout herself gave nothing away free — sitting up on the mounting stone by the moot-barn with her back against the wall, out at the edge of the lantern's reach, whittling a peg and letting the shavings fall between her boots. Tam Carter's father, whose road pair had just been read out from under him, said — not quite to anybody, in the direction of the mounting stone — that it was fine work, lying up in honest men's bracken, spying out beasts for the crown's book. The tale of the quarry-scrape had gone around the parish by dinner, improving as it went.

The knife didn't stop. The peg turned, took another shaving.

It was Seth Hodge who carried it forward. The warrener was a spare, deliberate man who had taken the wreath at the Ferrand fair shoots two years running, and he came at it civilly, as he did most things, with half the green quietly pushing at his back.

"That engine of yours," he said. "I'll say it plain, since nobody else will. I don't hold with them. A bow is a man's own arm and a man's own eye, trained up over years. A crossbow's a clerk's tool. Wind it, tick it, loose it. The engine does the knowing."

"There's a deal of that about," she said, to the peg.

It took a moment, and then it got a short laugh from the wrong end of the green, the end nearest the clerk. Hodge's jaw set. "Three ends at the long mark, then," he said, nodding at the old butts in the dark. "Your engine against my bow, and a shilling on it, and no hard feelings after."

The knife folded with a click. She looked at the butts for a moment, the grown-over mounds with the mist walking along them.

"A mark stands where you set it," she said, "and waits till you're ready. Nothing I'm sent after does that." She came down off the stone, and now the whole green was listening, which she seemed neither to seek nor to mind. "The long field, under the wold there, above the mere. The rooks come onto that stubble at first light — it's your parish, you'll know. And while the rest feed, there's always one up the thorn, watching."

"The sentry," Hodge said. "Aye."

"One bolt. The watch-bird, dead, inside thirty paces, before the drove walks tomorrow. Your shilling against mine."

There was a small silence of the kind that follows a price nobody had thought to hear. Hodge, being a hunter and a fair man, made the objection himself. "There's no coming at feeding rooks over open stubble. Not to a hundred paces, let be thirty. They post that watch for exactly this, and it counts every soul that comes near the field, and it'll have the flock up and gone while you're still two hedges—" He stopped, perhaps hearing what he had got to, which was explaining rooks to a poacher.

"Thirty paces," she said. "Have the shilling by you."

"Done, then. Done and witnessed."

"By the warden's blind eye." A poacher's oath, sworn level and public on the reeve's own green — and that got the laugh the evening had been needing, a real one, the whole crowd at once, while the reeve was careful to be studying the moot-barn roof and finding it in want of repair. The parish went up its lanes in better heart than it had come down them, with a match to talk about instead of a list, and if anybody but Joss wondered whether that trade had been offered on purpose, they kept it as quiet as he did. He couldn't make the notion sit square with the woman on the hillside, quite. He couldn't make it fall over, either.

At Fennstead the byre was lit. Joss came into the doorway and found his father with the body-brush, working the gelding over by lantern-light, long slow strokes down the quarter, the horse's lip loose and drooping with the pleasure of it. Bram didn't turn around. Joss took the other brush from the ledge and started on the off side, and the two of them worked in the small warm light with the cob pulling hay across the partition, and the only sounds were the brushes and the wind trying the door.

"Get his tail," Bram said, at last.

*  *  *

The morning came up grey off the mere with the mist lying in the field-bottoms like flood-water, and half the parish was at the low-road hedge before the light had properly sorted the thorn tree in the long field from the murk behind it. They had come to see the scout beaten, mostly, and some had simply come, the month being what it was; either way there was an edge of holiday to it that sat strangely on the day the horses went. Hodge had been at the field gate since the middle watch, by his own account, to see it fair, and had seen nothing come over the gate or through either hedge. Gret Dunmow was up a gatepost with her heels hooked in the bars, first of anyone to have claimed her seat.

The stubble ran pale away from the hedge, combed with the long windrows of raked leavings waiting on the plow, and the thorn stood mid-field, black and dripping. There was nothing else in the field.

"She's not come," Hodge said. "There's nothing in that field but straw, and I've watched it since the sky was stars."

The rooks came off the rookery elms behind the mill as the light thickened, talking as they came, and dropped onto the stubble in their dozens, and fell to. One rowed up onto the thorn and sat sentry, head going in small sharp turns, and the flock fed easy under its watching, and the hedge-crowd held still, and then, one by one, began to conclude that nothing whatever was going to happen, and to say so.

The crack came flat across the field, like a dry stick trodden on, and the watch-bird dropped out of the thorn without ceremony. The flock went up in a shout of wings and wheeled off toward the elms, crying the news, and in the middle of the long field a length of windrow stood up.

She rose out of the raked straw not ten strides from the thorn, shedding chaff, and stood there winding the crossbow with the same unhurry as everything else she did, while the hedge went up all at once, every voice and none of them finding words above an oath.

Hodge was over the gate before the flock had settled its argument in the elms. He walked out to the thorn and he did it like a juryman, pacing even from where she'd lain to where the bird had dropped, counting aloud for the hedge to hear. Eleven. He turned the rook with his boot, and looked at the bolt-hole in the neck of it, clean as an awl through leather, and stood up and took the shilling out of his coat and paid it over in front of the parish with a certain grim ceremony that did him credit.

"That's not shooting," he said, meaning more than one thing by it, and not all of them grudged.

"No," she agreed, and the coin went away somewhere about her coat.

"How long were you lying there?" Gret shouted, from the gatepost.

"Since the middle watch."

The crowd took that and worried at it — the middle watch, before ever Hodge came to the gate, six hours in raked straw with the dew falling on her, for a shilling — and somebody said the birds had come in right over her, and how was that, then?

"A flock counts what comes to the edges of a field once they're on it," she said. "They don't count the field." She drew her bolt, wiped it on the straw, picked the rook up by the feet, and on her way to the gate held it up to Gret on the gatepost. "Rook and onions. There's your seat paid for."

Joss stood at the hedge while the crowd began to move off, and looked at the field. He had watched it come out of the murk with a farmer's eye that knew it furrow by furrow — had cut barley over that ground himself not ten days since — and he had seen stubble, and windrows, and a thorn tree, and no third thing. The flattened length of straw lay mid-field now, plain as print once you knew to read it. *The shire has one of her,* he thought. *The Ascendancy will have its own.* He put the thought down. It didn't go far.

By midmorning the green was full of horses, and the holiday was over.

They came in along the lanes one at a time, led in halters that would be going home empty: the smith's dray mare stepping huge and gentle through a crowd she'd known all her life; Carter's pair, that half the green had borrowed a cart behind at one time or another; the Chessoms' riding cob, ridden in bareback by Marley to make the best of it. Col Aldery had somehow become the drovers' right hand within a quarter-hour of the string forming — the written men were let stand about the green all morning, it being nobody's interest to argue them back to work — and he went down the line of strange unsettled beasts with his slow hands and his slow voice, and where he'd been, they stood quieter. The crown had written him for the levy and was getting his morning free while it waited, and if Col saw it that way he gave no sign, having his hands on a frightened animal and being therefore, Joss judged, as content as the day was going to let him be.

Bram led the gelding in groomed to a shine, mane pulled, feet oiled, as if to a fair. The clerk never once looked at the shoulder where the ghost of the chalk still showed. He looked at his book, said, "Fennstead. Bay gelding, four years," made his tick, and waved up a drover, and Joss understood that the chalk had never been the mark at all: the mark had lived in the book from the day it was written, and the horse had only been wearing the notice of it.

The chit was written out, sanded, and handed over. Bram folded it in half unread and put it away in his coat, and his face through the whole transaction was the face he cut barley with. Lise had not come down — there were hens, she had said, and nobody had believed her, and nobody had said so. Petra stood at Joss's elbow and watched the clerk's pen, and said, quietly, to nobody who could mend it, "He'd fetch twice that at Ferrand fair."

Davin was at the gelding's head all the while with windfalls in both coat pockets, feeding them over one at a time. He'd been quiet all morning, and he stayed quiet while the drover came up and the halters were changed, Bram's coming back empty over Bram's shoulder. The last apple went over. Davin wiped his hand on his coat.

"Save me a stall, then," he said, and stepped back.

The string walked at noon: nine beasts of the parish and thirty-odd of the district's strung on ahead of them, up the lane to the war road, and east — over the toll, past the black yards where the hay that should have wintered them was ash, to Ferrand and then south to the depot at Marle, where the levy would train the horses first and the men after. Bram did not stay to watch them out of sight. He set off up the lane with the empty halter over his shoulder and his back straight, Petra falling in beside him, and Joss let them go ahead and stood on the emptying green, telling himself he was seeing the drove off, and knowing he was putting off walking into a yard with one horse in it.

The scout was still on the mounting stone. She had her stick out, and was cutting the morning's notches into it, small and even.

"You're not going with them," Joss said.

"The drove's the drovers' trouble now." She blew the cuttings off the stick and squinted along it. "I'm the shire's, up here, till the trains go south at the month's end. Roads to watch. There's more moving on them than there was."

He looked east, where the dust of the string still hung over the war road, thinning. His question came up the way his questions did, out of the middle of him, already grown. "Marley Chessom brings the courier-word up from Ferrand," he said. "All summer it's been the same word: the Seam holds. The lines sit where midsummer left them. My brother marches in six weeks. Is the word true?"

She didn't answer at once, and she didn't look up. She finished the notch she was cutting — the ninth, he saw, of a close-set group of nine — and turned the stick over in her fingers, once, the way he'd have done it himself.

"Couriers ride roads we hold," she said, "and sleep under roofs we still own, and they carry true word about the roads they ride. That's the trade, and there's no lie in it." She stood, and slung the crossbow, and said the rest the way she'd said the bracken was his, offhand, a fact among facts. "The Seam's worse than the couriers say. You heard of Brede because Brede got its people out to do the telling. There's towns that didn't."

She went off up the green toward the reeve's, not hurrying, the notched stick swinging at her belt, and left him standing with it. Away east the dust of the drove hung a while longer over the war road, going toward the toll and the rest of the world, and Joss stood on the trodden green with his hands empty.
