# Chapter One — Harvest

The purveyors had been counting since first light, and Joss had decided he didn't like the sound of it. Chalk on sackcloth, a tick and a scrape, over and over, drifting up from the yard below with the dust. It was the sound of somebody else's arithmetic being done on his family's winter.

He steadied the ladder with his boot and passed the chalk up to Wystan Crake, who stood on the third rung with his ear almost against the granary's main beam, listening to nothing.

"This one's gone quiet," Wystan said. "Held since the spring, mind. Good beam." He pressed two fingers flat to the wood, where a ladder of small angled marks climbed the grain like bird tracks. To Joss they looked the same as they had in spring. Wystan claimed a letter went dull to the touch when it faded, the way an egg went stale — nothing you could see, everything you could know.

"What does it feel like?" Joss asked. "When it's still holding."

"Busy." Wystan took the chalk and began to redraw the marks over their old ghosts, slow and exact, mouthing the shape-words as he went. "Like a hive in the wood. Now let me alone, this is the part where old men make mistakes."

Joss let him alone. Through the open gable door he could see most of the yard: sacks in their rows, the purveyor's man walking the rows with his slate, and Serjeant Cropp himself sitting on the mounting block with his bad leg out straight, adding figures in a leather book with the moot's stamp on the cover. Beyond the yard the stubble fields ran up the shoulder of the wold, pale as sand, and beyond those the sheep were a scatter of grey on the high ground, and everything looked the way it looked every year at cutting's end. Except for the counting.

The purveyor's man made another chalk cross. Tick, scrape.

Wystan finished the letter and sagged a little, one hand on the beam, and his next breath came out as fog. Midmorning, close air under a slate roof warm enough to bake in, and the old man's breath fogged like a January morning. Joss had seen it a hundred times and it still put a prickle up his neck. That was the price paid, Wystan said. You warmed the work with your own fire.

"Down," Wystan said. "Slow. If I fall on you, tell your mother it was her flour did it."

On the ground he wrapped both hands around the cup of hot mash Joss had ready — hot mash in harvest weather, and him in his winter coat — and drank, and shivered once, and was himself again. He squinted through the door at the yard.

"Heavier this year," he said.

"Cropp says a fifth."

"He said a seventh last year."

"I can count," Joss said. "So can Mam. She's been counting since they rode in."

"Aye, well." Wystan turned his cup. "There's a war on, they tell me."

There had been a war on for two years, and for two years it had stayed where it belonged, which was somewhere else. It was a thing couriers were about, and prices. It was the reason salt cost what it cost, and the reason the smith kept his best iron under the floor, and the reason the old east road — which nobody in Harrowmere had ever called anything but the war road, all Joss's life, though he'd never learned why — carried more traffic in a month now than it once had in a season. Wagons east, always east. Remounts in strings. Once, in the spring, a company of foot with a choir of twelve in grey coats marching in the middle of the column like a held breath.

Joss had watched them out of sight that day, and turned the thought of them over for a week the way he turned a strange stone up out of a furrow. Twelve people who were somehow one instrument. He'd asked Wystan how it worked and got half an answer, which from Wystan meant he didn't know either and was too honest to dress it.

"Letters in the loft to do yet," Wystan said. "And the malt-house. And the bounds to walk before the frosts, if my knees consent to the honor. And then I'll want dinner, and you'll want your father's field, or he'll want you at it." He looked sideways at Joss, the way he did when he was about to say a thing he'd decided not to say after all. "Hold the ladder."

*  *  *

They cut the last of the barley in the long field above the mere, all five of them and the Aldery brothers besides, and by midafternoon the field stood in stooks and the year's work was in sight of done. It was good work in good weather, and Joss lost himself in the swing of it for an hour at a stretch, which was the best thing about harvest: it emptied your head and filled your back, and questions kept for later.

His father worked the row ahead, steady as a millwheel. Bram Fenn was not a big man but he was a fixed one; he cut barley the way he did everything, as if he had agreed with it beforehand and there was nothing further to discuss. Davin worked wide on the left, going too fast because the Aldery boys were watching, leaving a ragged edge because he was going too fast. He was three years older than Joss, a head taller, and lucky the way some men are tall: it had been given him whole, early, and he would leave ragged edges his whole life and be forgiven every one.

Petra brought the water jug up at the turn of the row and didn't leave when she'd poured. She stood looking down the slope at the yard, where the purveyors' cart was being loaded with the first of the crown's fifth.

"He wrote our tally wrong," she said. "Cropp's man. He put down forty-one of the winter wheat and it's forty-three, I watched both loads."

"Then it's forty-one," Bram said, without stopping.

"But it's not."

"It is if it's written."

Petra looked at Joss with fourteen years of opinion in her face. She'd had her letters two years now — the reading kind; Wystan said she'd have taken to the other kind too, if the guild school weren't a shire away and dear — and she held the written word to a standard the written word rarely met. Joss tipped his head toward the farmhouse, half an inch, and she went, and took the argument with her for later use.

They were shocking the last stooks when the horses came up. Cropp's man led both of the Fenns' horses out along the headland — the old cob and the young gelding — walking them slow, studying their legs. At the field gate he chalked a mark high on the gelding's shoulder, a cross in a circle, and left the cob unmarked, and led them back down.

Davin straightened. "What's the mark?"

"Assessment," Joss said. The word had been going around the village all week, wearing a groove. "Means they've written him down. Means if the levy's trains come up short in autumn, they know where he stands."

"He's four years old. Da paid the covering fee on him two springs back, he's not—" Davin stopped himself. There was no one in the field to argue with. That was the trick of it, Joss thought; there never was anyone to argue with. There was a man with chalk, and a man with a slate, and a serjeant with a book, and somewhere far off at the end of all the writing there was a war, and no door in between that a man could knock on.

Bram had not straightened and not stopped cutting.

"Barley first," he said.

*  *  *

The Inning was held in the moot-barn as it always was, on the first clear evening after the last field came in, and it began the way it had begun for longer than anyone could say: the oldest hand present stood up with a heel of the new bread and said the grace.

This year the oldest was Widow Aldery, ninety-one and pleased about it. She held the bread up in both hands, and the barn went still, benches and rafters and sixty-odd people and the smell of roast mutton making a strong case against patience.

"For the hand on the plow," she said, "and the bread of it. We are quiet."

"We are quiet," said the barn, and then was anything but.

Joss ate until he had to change how he was sitting. There was mutton and there were geese; there was Lise Fenn's pear pie, which had a reputation across three parishes and deserved it; there was the first of the new mead, which Davin drank too much of on the grounds that the Aldery boys were drinking too much of it. The fiddle came out. Old Dunmow the miller sang the song about the ewe in the well, and everyone came in on the chorus and nobody remembered the fourth verse, same as every year, and the failure was part of the song by now, an inheritance.

Later, when the dancing had thinned the benches and the youngest children had gone glassy by the fire, Joss found Wystan in the corner with a half-circle of them at his feet, telling the story. He always told it at the Inning. It belonged to the night the way the grace did.

"Once," Wystan said, "the sky had more lights in it than it has now. You know how you see the stars, of a clear night? Well. The Bright Lords hung lamps below the stars, lamps the size of barns, and no one under them ever stubbed a toe in the dark, and no bread ever staled under that light, and no one was ever lost. Roads carried folk in those days like a river carries a leaf. Cities stood up taller than the wold and never fell down." He had a chestnut in his fingers, and he turned it as he talked, and Joss, standing behind the children, noticed his own hand turning an imaginary one. "And then the Discord came into the world. And of everything under the lamps there was one thing only it could not bear, and that was the lamps themselves. Envy is a cold house, children. It could not warm it. So it went up and down the world putting the lamps out, one and one and one, until the sky held nothing but the stars you see now — and the Bright Lords wept, and where the lamps had hung there was only the Silence, and the long night came, and that was the end of the elder world." He cracked the chestnut. "So when your mam tells you to bank the fire and you do it well, you've done a better thing than all the Bright Lords managed, for your lamp's still lit. Off to bed."

It got the groan it always got. One of the Dunmow girls, too young to know the shape of the telling, asked what the Discord looked like, and Wystan said what he always said — "Like whatever's behind you when you don't look" — and that was the end of the story and nearly the end of the evening, and it would have been a good one, one of the best Innings in years. People said so afterward, guiltily, for the rest of the autumn: it had been a good night, right up until the door.

The door opened for Marley Chessom, the reeve's nephew, who clerked at the shire town three days a week. He was dusty to the waist and he had his hat in one hand and a leather wallet in the other, and he stood in the doorway a moment against the dark, looking for someone. The fiddle finished its figure and did not start the next one.

"Reeve here?" Marley said, and then, because his uncle was already coming, he said the rest of it to the room at large, in the flat voice of a man who has practiced saying a thing so it will not shake. "Levy writs. Come down from the moot at Ferrand this morning. Three for this parish."

The barn had gone quiet again, and it was nothing like the grace.

Joss stood where he was, near the children and the fire, and watched his family across the room the way you watch weather come over the wold: Lise setting down the jug she held, wiping her hands on her apron although her hands were not wet; Bram turning from the trestle, unhurried, agreed-beforehand; Davin by the mead cask with the Alderys, laughing at something a half-second stale, the laugh drying on his face.

Reeve Chessom took the wallet and broke its string and read the writs to himself first, lips moving, which was cruel without meaning to be — six families watching one man's lips. Then he read them aloud, in the order they were written, which was no order at all: names drawn out of the shire's ledgers by a clerk who had never seen Harrowmere.

"Col Aldery, son of Josten Aldery, smith."

A sound from the smith's bench, low, one syllable, bitten off. Col himself — twenty, wide as the door, gentlest hand with a horse in the parish — looked down at his own hands on the table as if they'd been named separately.

"Edmund Dunmow, son of Aldous Dunmow, miller."

Edd was at the back with the fiddler. He said, clear in the silence, "Well, there's the fourth verse found," and it was a terrible joke and nobody laughed and several people loved him for it, Joss among them, forever.

"Davin Fenn, son of Bram Fenn, of Fennstead."

Joss discovered he had already known. From the moment the door opened; maybe from the moment the chalk went on the gelding's shoulder; maybe, unfairly, from all summer, the way a man knows which way a leaning tree will fall and still stands watching it. He looked at his brother and his brother looked at nothing at all for the length of one long breath — at the doorway and the dark in it — and then Davin Fenn straightened up and smiled his ragged-edge smile and said, loud, "Mind you keep the gelding fed, then, they've written us down together," and reached for the cup he'd sworn off two rounds back.

After that the Inning did what gatherings do when a thing lands in the middle of them: it closed around the hole. The fiddle started again, slower. Women went to Lise; men went to Bram and stood near him without saying much, which was the wold's way of shouting. The reeve wrote the delivery date in his uncle's book — the eighth of the month after next, at the shire town, kit and shoes provided, the crown's shilling on arrival — and Marley Chessom drank a cup standing and rode out for the next parish with two more writs in the wallet, and the night, which had been a good night, went on being a night.

Joss carried sleeping children to wagons. It was the job you did at his age if you weren't dancing, and it was better than standing still.

His mother found him at it, out in the yard between trips, and put the last of the pear pie into his hands wrapped in a cloth, for no reason he could name and neither could she. Lise Fenn was a small woman who had never once been between Joss and anything without his noticing it there, and she looked older tonight by exactly one evening, and no more; she would not give the shire the satisfaction.

"Fifty-one days," she said, as if he'd asked. "He'll want two shirts sewn, and his boots are your father's old ones and they won't do for marching." She looked back at the lit barn, the shapes moving over the doorway's spill of light, the fiddle working through it. "Eat that before your brother sees it."

She went in. Joss stood in the dark holding warm pie like a small coal, and found his eyes stinging, and blamed the night air, and knew better.

*  *  *

He couldn't have said what woke him, or if he'd slept. The loft window of the farmhouse showed a sky an hour or more past midnight, and Davin's bed was empty, and the ladder to the yard was creaking its third rung, which it did for no one's weight but Davin's.

Joss found him where he knew he'd find him, up the outside stair of the great barn and out along the roof ridge, where the two of them had sat on a hundred summer nights being told to come down. He was still in his Inning shirt. He had the mead cup with him, empty, and he was turning it in his hands, which was Joss's habit and not his, and Joss climbed the slates and sat, and neither of them said anything for a while. The mere lay flat and pale under the moon. An owl worked the water meadows. Away over the wold's shoulder, small as a spark, the light at the Ferrand toll kept burning, as it always had, where the old east road went down the far slope and set out for the rest of the world.

"I'll be back by lambing," Davin said at last. "They train you the winter through, is what Col's father heard. Down at the depot at Marle. Then it's garrison, like as not — the Concord pays for Harthen boys to stand on Concord walls, that's the whole shape of the treaty, everyone says so. Garrison and drill and mud. I'll be back by lambing with the crown's shillings and a talent for cards."

"Which lambing," Joss said.

Davin turned the cup over. "You're a cold comfort, little brother."

"You'd have caught me lying."

"I'd have thanked you for it." But he knocked his shoulder into Joss's as he said it, and left it there, and they sat leaning like that in the quiet. Below them in the paddock the marked gelding shifted and blew, unwritten-down in his own opinion, four years old under the whole of the sky.

Joss was thinking about the choir he'd seen in the spring, the twelve in grey walking like one thing, and was about to say something about it — he didn't know what, only that it had been sitting in him since morning, since the letter that was a hive in the wood, and it had Davin's name on it now and needed saying — when Davin went still against his shoulder.

"What's that."

He was looking east. Joss looked east.

The war road ran up out of the river valley three miles off, crossed the wold's shoulder at the Ferrand toll, and went down the other side, and past the toll-light there should have been nothing but night: no farm on that stretch, no charcoal camp, nothing to burn. Low above the black line of the hills there was a glow. It was not a big glow. That was the thing Joss fixed on, after — how modest it was, how easily a man could have blinked and called it moonrise or a trick of tired eyes, if it had been the season for moonrise in the east, which it wasn't; if it had wavered like marsh-light, which it didn't. It sat on the horizon in one place, orange as a banked coal, and above it the smoke showed as a long smudge against the stars, leaning north with the night wind. While they watched, a second light woke beside the toll-spark — small, moving — and then another. Riders' lanterns, going fast, the way nobody rode that road at night.

"Something's burning on the war road," Davin said. His voice had nothing in it, like Marley Chessom's in the barn door. Practiced flat, over a shake.

"Aye," Joss said.

The word came out level. His hands had gone cold on the slates.

They sat and watched it burn, the two of them, until the toll-light and the lanterns and the coal-glow swam together in the east, and neither one said what both were thinking, which was that the war had always stayed where it belonged, and that somewhere in the dark past Ferrand it had stopped doing that; and that the eighth of the month after next was fifty-one days off; and that a man could stand and watch a leaning tree his whole life, and still be under it when it came down.

They went in before dawn. The glow outlasted them.
